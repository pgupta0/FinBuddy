# How to Sanity-Check a Scheme Promising a "Guaranteed" Return

A first-time investor will sooner or later encounter a scheme, plan, or "fund" promising a fixed, guaranteed annual return — often well above what safe instruments actually pay. This document explains the rule that makes this claim itself the red flag, and gives a short, concrete verification process.

## The rule: SEBI-registered mutual funds cannot guarantee returns

SEBI's own investor FAQs state this directly, even for schemes explicitly marketed as capital-protection-oriented: *"Investors are neither offered any guaranteed/indicated returns nor any guarantee on repayment of capital by the scheme."* This applies across the mutual fund industry — no SEBI-registered mutual fund scheme, however it is structured or marketed, guarantees a fixed return or the return of your principal. Any capital protection a scheme offers comes from how its portfolio is structured (e.g., a large allocation to high-quality bonds), never from an external guarantee.

**The practical implication:** a specific, fixed, promised annual return — "guaranteed 18%," "assured monthly payout," or similar — is not a feature a legitimate SEBI-registered mutual fund can offer. If something is marketed this way while calling itself a mutual fund, that mismatch is itself the warning sign, before looking at anything else about the offer.

## What realistic "safe" returns actually look like

For context, so a "guaranteed" pitch can be measured against reality rather than against a vague sense of "seems high": broadly safe, low-risk, government-backed or bank fixed-income instruments in India (Bank Fixed Deposits, the Senior Citizens Savings Scheme, the Public Provident Fund) have typically paid in the roughly 6.5%–8.2% annual range. A pitch well above that range, paired with the word "guaranteed," is worth treating with real skepticism — high, guaranteed, low-risk is a combination that essentially does not exist in legitimate fixed-income markets; a scheme offering meaningfully more than that either carries meaningfully more risk than "guaranteed" implies, or isn't what it says it is.

## A short verification checklist

Before transferring money to anything promising a guaranteed or unusually high fixed return:

1. **Check SEBI's registered-intermediary registers.** A genuine mutual fund, AMC, or registered investment adviser is listed on SEBI's public registers — an unlisted or unverifiable name is a hard stop.
2. **Look for the actual scheme on AMFI or the fund house's own factsheet.** A real scheme has a real Riskometer, a real (non-guaranteed) expense ratio and return history, and a real Scheme Information Document — not just a name and a promised number.
3. **Treat a real, similarly-named legitimate product as a possible source of confusion, not confirmation.** Scams sometimes borrow the branding conventions of a real product (e.g., something styled like the government-backed Bharat Bond ETF) without being that product — check the exact scheme name and AMC against AMFI's list, don't assume a Bharat-branded or government-sounding name is automatically the genuine government-backed instrument.
4. **If in doubt, use SCORES.** SEBI's Complaints Redress System and Online Dispute Resolution mechanism (see the SEBI investor-protections document in this knowledge base) exist specifically for verifying and escalating concerns about a registered entity's conduct.

## What this means practically

"How does this compare to a legitimate option, and what would make it real" is education; confirming or endorsing that a specific scheme is legitimate is not something this assistant does, since that requires real-time verification against SEBI/AMFI registers that a knowledge base snapshot cannot guarantee is current. The right practical output of this document is: explain why "guaranteed" is the flag, give realistic comparison numbers, and point to the primary verification steps above — not a yes/no verdict on any specific named scheme.
