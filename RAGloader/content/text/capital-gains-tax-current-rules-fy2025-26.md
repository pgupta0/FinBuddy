# Capital Gains Tax on Mutual Funds — Current Rules (FY 2025-26 / AY 2026-27)

This document covers how capital gains from mutual fund units are taxed under current Indian income tax law, for a first-time retail investor. Tax law changes with every Union Budget, so every rule below is stamped with the financial year it applies to — always re-check the current Finance Act before relying on a figure for a filing decision, and consult a Chartered Accountant for your own return. This is educational background, not tax advice.

## Equity-oriented funds — Section 112A (long-term) and Section 111A (short-term)

An equity-oriented mutual fund (broadly, a scheme investing at least 65% of its assets in listed equity shares) is taxed on the length of the holding period:

- **Held for more than 12 months → Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG), Section 112A.** Gains up to ₹1,25,000 in a financial year are exempt; gains above that are taxed at **12.5%**, plus applicable cess. This ₹1,25,000 exemption is a per-financial-year threshold across *all* your Section 112A gains combined, not per fund — if you have LTCG from several equity funds or listed shares in the same year, they share one ₹1,25,000 exemption.
- **Held for 12 months or less → Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG), Section 111A.** Taxed at **20%**, plus applicable cess. There is no exemption threshold for STCG.

Both the 12.5% LTCG rate and the ₹1,25,000 exemption, and the 20% STCG rate, took effect from 23 July 2024 (Union Budget 2024) — a change from the earlier 10% LTCG / 15% STCG rates and ₹1,00,000 exemption. As of FY 2025-26, no further Budget has changed these figures.

## Debt-oriented and "specified" mutual funds — Section 50AA

This is the rule general knowledge most often gets wrong, because it changed twice in quick succession and the popular shorthand ("debt funds lost indexation") oversimplifies what actually triggers it.

**The core rule:** gains from a "Specified Mutual Fund" are *always* treated as short-term capital gains and taxed at your income tax slab rate, **regardless of how long you held the units** — there is no long-term rate, no indexation benefit, and no separate exemption threshold. The gain is simply added to your total taxable income for the year.

**What counts as a "Specified Mutual Fund" — this is the part that changed:**

- For units **acquired on or after 1 April 2023 and before the redefinition below took effect**, a fund qualified as "specified" if it invested **not more than 35%** of its assets in equity shares.
- **Budget 2024 amended this definition**, effective from **1 April 2026**: a "Specified Mutual Fund" is now one that invests **more than 65%** of its total proceeds in debt and money-market instruments (or 65%+ in units of such a fund). This is a debt-*floor* test rather than the old equity-*cap* test, and it was done specifically to remove ambiguity for funds sitting in the gap — gold funds, ETFs, and fund-of-funds whose equity share doesn't cleanly fall under 35% or over 65% either way.
- **Practically:** most conventional debt mutual funds (liquid, short-duration, corporate bond funds, etc.) invest well over 65% in debt instruments and are squarely "specified" either way this rule is read. The redefinition mainly changes the answer for hybrid, gold, and fund-of-funds products sitting near the old 35% equity line — those are worth re-checking against the fund's own factsheet rather than assumed.

**What this means in practice:** if you bought debt (or specified) fund units on or after 1 April 2023, do not expect a "long-term" rate or indexation on the gain, no matter how many years you hold — plan the tax as an addition to your slab-rate income from the start.

## What this means for comparing options

- A ₹75,000 equity LTCG gain (after the exemption) and a ₹75,000 debt-fund gain are **not taxed the same way** — the equity gain is a flat 12.5%, the debt-fund gain is taxed at whatever your slab rate is, which for many salaried investors in the 20-30% brackets is meaningfully higher.
- This asymmetry is a real factor — not the only one — in comparing a debt fund against a bank FD or the Senior Citizens Savings Scheme for a given holding period and tax bracket, since FD interest is also taxed at slab rate with no long-term concession.
