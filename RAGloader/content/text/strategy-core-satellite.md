# Strategy Framework: Core-Satellite, Explained Simply

## The core idea

A core-satellite portfolio splits money into two conceptually different parts. The **core** is a
large, stable, low-cost, broadly diversified holding — typically a large-cap index fund or a
diversified equity/debt combination — intended to provide steady, reliable, market-matching exposure
with minimal ongoing decision-making. The **satellites** are a smaller number of more targeted,
higher-conviction, or more actively managed holdings — a sector fund, a mid- or small-cap fund, a
specific theme — layered around the core in an attempt to add extra return or exposure to a specific
opportunity, without risking the whole portfolio's stability if any one satellite underperforms.

## How it works in practice

1. Decide the core-versus-satellite split — commonly 70–80% core and 20–30% satellite, though the
   exact split depends on an investor's risk profile and conviction level; a more conservative
   investor might use a larger core and smaller, fewer satellites.
2. Fill the core with low-cost, broad, diversified funds — a Nifty 50 or Nifty 500 index fund, or a
   diversified large-cap/flexi-cap fund — chosen for stability and low expense ratio rather than for
   the chance of dramatically beating the market.
3. Fill the satellites with a small number of more specific bets — a sector or thematic fund, a
   mid-cap or small-cap fund for extra growth potential, or a specific tactical position — sized small
   enough that even a significant underperformance in one satellite has limited impact on the whole
   portfolio.
4. Rebalance periodically: since satellites are meant to be higher-conviction and can grow to become
   a larger share of the portfolio than intended if they perform very well (or shrink if they perform
   poorly), the core-satellite split itself needs occasional rebalancing back toward the target ratio.

## Where the idea comes from

Core-satellite investing grew out of practices originally used by institutional investors and
financial advisers as a structured way to combine passive, low-cost core exposure with the potential
for active outperformance in a controlled, risk-limited way, rather than choosing exclusively between
"all passive" and "all active." See [Wikipedia — Core & Satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_%26_Satellite) and [Saxo — The Core-Satellite Approach](https://www.home.saxo/learn/guides/diversification/core-satellite-approach-a-smarter-way-to-diversify-your-investments) for background.

## What it's trying to solve

Choosing only actively managed funds across an entire portfolio concentrates cost and manager-specific
risk across everything an investor owns. Choosing only passive index funds gives up any chance of
benefiting from a specific view or opportunity. Core-satellite is a structured middle path: most of
the portfolio gets the low-cost, reliable, market-matching behavior of index-style investing, while a
clearly bounded portion is set aside for higher-conviction ideas — so a wrong call on a satellite
cannot derail the whole portfolio.

## Who this framework tends to suit

An investor who wants exposure to specific opportunities or sectors they have a view on, or who wants
some potential for beating a plain market-matching return, but who also wants most of their money to
behave predictably and not be exposed to concentrated single-sector or single-strategy risk. It suits
someone who wants a structured way to "experiment" with a portion of their portfolio without risking
the whole plan.

## What it deliberately does not do

- It does not mean putting satellites in unrelated, purely speculative single-stock bets — satellites
  are still intended to be diversified fund-level positions, just more targeted than the core.
- It does not eliminate the need to choose a sensible core — a poorly diversified or high-cost "core"
  undermines the entire structure's purpose.
- It is not a guarantee that satellites will outperform — they are a bounded, sized bet on a specific
  view, and that view can be wrong.
