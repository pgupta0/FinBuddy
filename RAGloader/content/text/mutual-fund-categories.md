# A Plain-English Guide to Mutual Fund Categories in India

In October 2017, SEBI (the Securities and Exchange Board of India) issued a categorization and
rationalization circular requiring every mutual fund house to classify its schemes into a common set
of defined categories, so that a scheme called, say, "Large Cap Fund" means broadly the same thing at
every fund house, and investors can compare like with like. Fund houses are allowed only one scheme
per category (with a few exceptions), which also reduced the earlier problem of overlapping,
confusingly similar scheme names. Sources: [AMFI — Categorization of Mutual Fund Schemes](https://www.amfiindia.com/investor/knowledge-center-info?zoneName=CategorizationOfMutualFundSchemes), [HDFC Mutual Fund — SEBI's Mutual Fund Classifications Explained](https://www.hdfcfund.com/learn/blog/sebi-mandated-mutual-fund-classifications).

There are five broad groups, each with defined subcategories.

## 1. Equity Schemes

Equity schemes invest mainly in shares of companies, and are generally the highest-volatility,
highest-long-term-growth-potential category among the five. Key subcategories:

- **Large Cap Fund** — invests mainly in the top 100 companies by market capitalization (i.e., the
  largest, most established, generally most stable listed companies).
- **Mid Cap Fund** — invests mainly in companies ranked roughly 101st to 250th by market
  capitalization — smaller than large caps, with more growth potential but also more volatility.
- **Small Cap Fund** — invests mainly in companies ranked 251st and beyond by market capitalization —
  the smallest listed companies, with the highest growth potential and the highest volatility and risk
  among the three market-cap categories.
- **Multi Cap Fund** and **Flexi Cap Fund** — invest across large, mid, and small caps, with
  Multi Cap funds required to hold a minimum allocation to each of the three, while Flexi Cap funds
  have full flexibility to shift the mix.
- **ELSS (Equity Linked Savings Scheme)** — a tax-saving equity fund under Section 80C with a
  mandatory 3-year lock-in (see the glossary entry for details).
- **Sectoral / Thematic Funds** — concentrate on one industry (e.g., banking, IT, pharma) or one
  theme (e.g., infrastructure, consumption) — higher concentration risk than a diversified equity fund.
- **Value Fund** and **Contra Fund** — follow a specific investment style, buying companies the
  manager believes are undervalued relative to their fundamentals.
- **Dividend Yield Fund**, **Focused Fund** (concentrated in a smaller number of stocks), and
  **Equity Income / Dividend Yield** variants also exist within this group.

## 2. Debt Schemes

Debt schemes invest in fixed-income instruments — government securities, corporate bonds, money
market instruments — that pay a defined interest rate. They are generally lower-volatility than
equity, though not risk-free (they carry interest-rate risk and credit risk). Key subcategories, from
shortest to longest typical maturity:

- **Overnight Fund** and **Liquid Fund** — invest in instruments maturing within a day to 91 days;
  used as a parking place for money needed very soon, similar in purpose to a savings account but
  usually with a somewhat better return.
- **Ultra Short Duration**, **Low Duration**, **Short Duration**, **Medium Duration**, **Medium to
  Long Duration**, and **Long Duration Funds** — categorized by the average maturity of the bonds
  they hold, from a few months to several years; generally, longer duration means more sensitivity to
  interest rate changes (both up and down).
- **Corporate Bond Fund** — invests at least 80% of assets in the highest-rated corporate bonds.
- **Credit Risk Fund** — deliberately invests in lower-rated corporate bonds for a higher yield,
  taking on meaningfully more credit (default) risk in exchange.
- **Gilt Fund** — invests only in government securities, which carry no credit risk (the government
  is not expected to default) but still carry interest-rate risk.
- **Banking and PSU Fund** — invests mainly in debt instruments of banks, public sector undertakings,
  and similar entities.
- **Dynamic Bond Fund** — the fund manager actively adjusts the portfolio's duration based on their
  interest-rate outlook, rather than sticking to one fixed duration band.

## 3. Hybrid Schemes

Hybrid schemes mix equity and debt in the same scheme, in varying proportions, to balance growth
potential against stability. Key subcategories:

- **Conservative Hybrid Fund** — roughly 10–25% equity, 75–90% debt; oriented toward stability with a
  small equity kicker.
- **Balanced Hybrid Fund** — roughly 40–60% equity, 40–60% debt.
- **Aggressive Hybrid Fund** — roughly 65–80% equity, 20–35% debt; oriented toward growth with a debt
  cushion.
- **Multi Asset Allocation Fund** — invests across at least three asset classes (e.g., equity, debt,
  and gold), with a minimum allocation to each.
- **Arbitrage Fund** — see the glossary entry; technically hybrid, but behaves like a low-risk,
  short-term debt-like investment.
- **Equity Savings Fund** — combines equity, debt, and arbitrage positions to reduce net equity market
  exposure while retaining some of equity's tax treatment.
- **Balanced Advantage Fund / Dynamic Asset Allocation Fund** — automatically shifts the equity-debt
  mix based on market valuation signals, rather than holding a fixed proportion.

## 4. Solution-Oriented Schemes

Designed around a specific life goal rather than a general market segment, and structured with a
lock-in to encourage the discipline needed to actually reach that goal:

- **Retirement Fund** — a minimum 5-year lock-in, or until retirement age, whichever is earlier.
- **Children's Fund** — a minimum 5-year lock-in, or until the child turns 18, whichever is earlier.

## 5. Other Schemes

Everything that does not fit the growth-vs-income framing of the first three groups:

- **Index Funds / ETFs** — passively track a specific market index (see the glossary entry on Index
  Funds and ETFs).
- **Fund of Funds (FoF)** — invests in units of other mutual funds rather than directly in securities
  (see glossary).
- **Gold ETF / Gold Fund** — gives exposure to gold prices without needing to buy and store physical
  gold.
- **International Fund of Funds** — invests in mutual funds or ETFs based overseas, giving exposure to
  foreign markets.

## The Riskometer, applied to categories

Every scheme, regardless of category, must display a Riskometer reading — one of Low, Low to
Moderate, Moderate, Moderately High, High, or Very High — using SEBI's standardized methodology. As a
rough pattern (always check the specific scheme's actual current Riskometer rather than assuming):
liquid and overnight debt funds tend to sit at the Low to Moderate end; most diversified debt funds
sit in the Moderate range; hybrid funds sit around Moderately High; and diversified equity, sector,
and small-cap funds sit at High to Very High. See [SEBI Investor Website — Riskometer](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/riskometer.html) for the full methodology.

## Why this matters for choosing a strategy

The three strategy frameworks this assistant discusses — risk parity, core-satellite, and
endowment-style — are all, at their core, different disciplined approaches to combining schemes from
these categories (plus, for the institutional-style frameworks, asset classes beyond what a retail
mutual fund investor typically accesses directly) in proportions designed to match a specific
objective. Understanding what each category actually contains is what makes those allocation
percentages meaningful rather than abstract.
