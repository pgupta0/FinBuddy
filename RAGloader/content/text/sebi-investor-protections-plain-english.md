# SEBI's Investor Protections, in Plain English

SEBI (the Securities and Exchange Board of India) is the regulator for India's securities markets,
including mutual funds and investment advisers. This document summarizes, in plain language, the
protections and rules most relevant to a first-time retail investor. It is educational background,
not legal advice, and specific rules should always be checked against SEBI's own current
circulars and the specific scheme's factsheet, since regulations are periodically updated.

## The Investor Charter

SEBI publishes an Investor Charter (separate charters exist for mutual funds, investment advisers, and
other market segments) that lays out, in one place, what an investor is entitled to expect and what an
investor is expected to do. See [SEBI Investor Website — Investor Charter](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/Investor-charter.html).

**Investor rights include:**
- Fair and equitable treatment from every SEBI-registered intermediary (fund houses, distributors,
  advisers, brokers).
- Access to clear, adequate, and accurate information before investing (scheme documents, factsheets,
  the Riskometer, expense ratios) and quality ongoing service afterward.
- Time-bound grievance redressal — a complaint filed through SEBI's SCORES portal (SEBI Complaints
  Redress System) must be addressed within defined timelines.
- Access to an Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) mechanism if a grievance is not resolved through the
  regular channel.
- The right to exit an investment or a service relationship on fair and reasonable terms.

**Investor responsibilities include:**
- Deal only with SEBI-registered or SEBI-recognized entities — always verify registration before
  investing or taking advice (SEBI maintains public registers of registered intermediaries).
- Raise grievances with the concerned entity within the prescribed time limits, and escalate to SCORES
  if unresolved.
- Keep investment accounts and folios used only for one's own benefit — never lend an account or KYC
  identity to someone else.
- Complete KYC (identity and address verification) truthfully and keep it updated.

## Who is allowed to give "investment advice," and what that means

SEBI's Investment Advisers Regulations, 2013 require anyone providing investment advisory services
for consideration (a fee) to retail or institutional clients to register with SEBI as a Registered
Investment Adviser (RIA), subject to specific exemptions. This matters for a first-time investor
because it determines what kind of relationship they actually have with whoever is giving them
guidance. See [FAQs on SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013](https://taxguru.in/sebi/sebi-investment-adviser-regulations-applicable-me.html).

**Registration as an RIA is generally required** for anyone whose core business is giving personalized
investment recommendations in exchange for a fee.

**Common exemptions from RIA registration** (the advice must be incidental to another regulated or
professional activity, not a standalone advisory business) include:
- Mutual fund distributors, when the "advice" they give is incidental to helping a client buy or sell
  a fund — distributors are compensated by commission from the fund house, not a client fee, which is
  a structurally different (and potentially conflicted) incentive than a fee-only adviser's.
- General commentary distributed through media (newspapers, TV, widely available publications) that
  does not target a specific client's individual situation.
- Insurance agents/brokers advising only on insurance products, and pension advisers registered with a
  pension regulator, when advising within that scope.
- Lawyers, Chartered Accountants, Company Secretaries, and actuaries, when investment-related advice
  is incidental to their primary professional service.
- Stockbrokers, portfolio managers, and merchant bankers already registered with SEBI, for advice
  incidental to their registered activity.

**Why this matters for an educational tool:** a tool that explains general strategy frameworks and
lets a user see how their own already-existing holdings compare to a framework is functioning
differently from a tool that tells a specific person to buy or sell a specific security based on their
individual circumstances — the latter is squarely what RIA registration exists to regulate. This is
also why this assistant is explicit that it is not a registered investment adviser and does not give
personalized advice: it discusses general frameworks and comparisons, not individualized
buy/sell/hold recommendations.

## Mutual fund cost transparency rules

- SEBI requires every mutual fund scheme to disclose its Total Expense Ratio (TER) and to cap it
  according to slabs that decrease as the scheme's assets under management grow (see the glossary
  entry on TER for illustrative slab figures — always verify the specific current cap on the scheme's
  factsheet, since SEBI periodically revises the slab structure).
- SEBI requires every scheme to offer a Direct Plan alongside its Regular Plan, so investors always
  have the option to invest without paying a distribution commission if they do not want or need
  advisory support from a distributor.
- Every scheme must display a standardized Riskometer using SEBI's common methodology (see the
  glossary and the mutual fund categories document), so risk levels are comparable across fund
  houses.

## What this means practically for a first-time investor

- Check who is registered and how they are compensated (fee vs. commission) before treating guidance
  as personalized advice.
- Compare the Direct Plan expense ratio against the Regular Plan for the same scheme before deciding
  which to use, especially for a long holding period where the compounding effect of the cost
  difference is largest.
- Use the Riskometer as a first filter before reading further into any scheme, since it is standardized
  and mandatory.
- Use SCORES and the ODR mechanism if a genuine grievance with a registered entity goes unresolved
  through normal channels.
