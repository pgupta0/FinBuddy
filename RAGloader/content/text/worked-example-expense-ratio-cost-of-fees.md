# Worked Example: What a Higher Expense Ratio Actually Costs

A common first-time-investor mistake is treating a Total Expense Ratio (TER) difference of "just 1%" as a rounding error. It isn't — TER is charged every year, on the whole invested balance, for as long as you hold the fund, so a small annual difference compounds into a large absolute cost over a long holding period. This document works the arithmetic out explicitly, with the formula shown, so the number is independently checkable rather than a claim to take on faith.

## The scenario

Two funds both track the same index (say, the Nifty 50), so their **gross** return before costs is assumed identical — the only difference between them is what each one charges:

- **Fund A:** TER 0.10% per year (typical of a low-cost direct-plan index fund)
- **Fund B:** TER 1.20% per year (typical of a higher-cost regular-plan or actively-marketed fund)
- Both assumed to deliver an identical **11% gross annual return**
- ₹5,00,000 invested as a lump sum, held for **20 years**

## The arithmetic

A fund's **net** return is its gross return minus its TER (this simplification ignores tracking error and the exact compounding mechanics of how a TER is deducted intraday, but is accurate enough to show the scale of the effect):

- Fund A net return: 11% − 0.10% = **10.90%** per year
- Fund B net return: 11% − 1.20% = **9.80%** per year

Using the standard compound growth formula FV = P × (1 + r)^n, with P = ₹5,00,000 and n = 20 years:

- **Fund A:** ₹5,00,000 × (1.109)^20 ≈ **₹39,59,000**
- **Fund B:** ₹5,00,000 × (1.098)^20 ≈ **₹32,44,000**

**Difference: roughly ₹7,15,000 — over ₹7.1 lakh — lost to the extra 1.1 percentage points of annual cost, on the exact same underlying index, over 20 years.** That's about 18% less final corpus for Fund B relative to Fund A, from cost alone, with zero difference in the actual investment performance of the two funds.

## Why the gap is bigger than "1% a year" sounds

The cost isn't just "1% of your money once a year" — it's 1% of a balance that would otherwise also have been compounding at the higher net rate. Every rupee the higher-cost fund charges in year 3 is a rupee that doesn't get 17 more years to grow. That's why a fee difference that looks small annually becomes large — often the single largest controllable factor in a long-horizon outcome — once compounded over a multi-decade holding period.

## What this means practically

- For two funds tracking the *same* index or benchmark, the TER difference is close to the entire explanation for a difference in their final value — there's no skill difference to justify paying more.
- This is exactly why SEBI requires every scheme to offer a Direct Plan (no distribution commission, lower TER) alongside its Regular Plan — see the SEBI investor-protections document in this knowledge base. Comparing Direct vs Regular TER for the same scheme, especially for a long holding period, is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-effort decisions a first-time investor can make.
- These figures are an illustrative, hand-computed projection assuming a constant gross return every year, which real markets never deliver — actual outcomes vary with real market performance. Treat this as an explanation of *why TER matters*, not a forecast of what any specific fund will return.
